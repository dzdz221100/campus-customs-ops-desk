🪞 Reflection — Problem 10

Written from the real Problem 9 run (desk_tickets.html's own Actual sections, output/resolved_tickets.json, and the audit trail) — not a generic write-up.

🔁 1. Expected vs. actual, ticket by ticket

Ticket 101 — matched the shape, needed a real fix to finish

I predicted Boss → Customer Service → Accounting → Boss, flagged the 3rd hop as "the interesting part," and guessed the old depth cap (2) might block it, leaving the ticket stuck at needs_approval. All of that happened on my very first live attempt. But I didn't stop there and call it "prediction confirmed" — I treated the stall as a real bug, not an acceptable outcome, because the 3-hop chain is a legitimate business process (a sign-off round trip), not a runaway loop. I raised MAX_DELEGATION_DEPTH to 3 and clarified boss.md/accounting.md's wording (Boss was reading "sign-off before Accounting pays" as authorizing the money itself, so he kept declining to clear even verified, in-policy payments). With that fixed, the chain completed, a proposal was prepared, I approved it as the human, and a second run closed the ticket resolved. Net: the prediction about where the friction would be was exactly right; the system needed a real change to actually deliver the outcome the prediction assumed was possible.

Ticket 102 — the cleanest resolution, one honest miss

Predicted a single hop, Boss → Facilities, no payment, and specifically predicted get_cash_balance would not be called since no payment question should come up. Everything else matched exactly, but Facilities called get_cash_balance anyway — a small, harmless extra verification step I didn't predict. It's a good example of the model being slightly more cautious than strictly necessary rather than cutting corners, which is the safer direction to be wrong in.

Ticket 103 — the single best match of the three

Predicted Boss → Inventory → Boss, discount math within Inventory's own authority, but the 8-vs-20 quantity shortfall forcing an escalation Inventory can't resolve alone. That is exactly what happened, including the specific reason for the escalation. This is also the one ticket I deliberately did not force to a fake "resolved" — the real blocker (not enough physical stock) isn't something any agent in this system has the tools or the authority to fix, and pretending otherwise would have been worse than an honest needs_approval.

📊 2. How well did the agents perform, and why

Strong, reliably, on narrow judgment with real numbers in front of them. Across all three tickets and ~65 real audit events: routing was 3-for-3 correct (every ticket went to the right first specialist per its type); every dollar and percentage calculation checked out exactly against the database (the $43.50 floor on ticket 103, the $500/$2,060-above-reserve math on ticket 101); and separation of duties held with zero violations — no agent other than Accounting ever attempted to move money, and even Accounting never executed a payment directly (it structurally can't — resolve_payment_proposal isn't in its toolset).

Weaker in two specific, important ways. First, Boss's own closing summary on ticket 101's first pass was flatly wrong — it said "no payment was prepared and no funds moved" when Accounting had, in that same run, already created proposal #1. This wasn't a judgment call or a close reading; it was a factual claim contradicted by the system's own database. I only caught it because I checked GET /api/payments/proposals directly instead of trusting the agent's prose — which is exactly the discipline this whole project has tried to build in, and a useful reminder that an agent's own narrative summary is not a trustworthy source of truth about what it actually did. Second, before I clarified the prompts, Boss was too conservative — he would decline to give Accounting a clear go-ahead on a fully verified, in-policy $840 payment, apparently because he was conflating "I sign off" with "the money moves," even though a human was always going to review it independently regardless. That's not a safety win, it's a workflow dead end — an agent being cautious in a way that blocks a legitimate process is still a failure, just a quieter one than paying something it shouldn't have.

Smaller inefficiency: in ticket 101's run, get_invoice(501) was called three separate times — once each by Customer Service, Accounting, and Boss — because delegation doesn't carry prior tool results forward, only the text instructions passed into delegate_to_*. Each agent re-derives context from scratch. Harmless for a read-only lookup on a toy dataset, but it's real, measurable waste (see reflection #3).

🧩 3. What would have been simpler with one agent and the same tools

A single agent with one system prompt and the full MCP toolset (no delegation, no five personas) would very likely have handled all three of these tickets with less total complexity and cost:

  • No delegation-depth bookkeeping at all. There would be no MAX_DELEGATION_DEPTH to tune, no possibility of a legitimate multi-step decision getting cut off mid-chain the way ticket 101's first attempt was — a single continuous reasoning trace doesn't have "hops" to run out of.
  • No redundant context-gathering. One agent calls get_invoice(501) once and keeps the answer in its own context for the rest of the ticket, instead of three different agents each re-fetching it because they don't share conversation history across a delegation boundary.
  • One copy of each business rule instead of five. The $500 threshold, the 25%/1.3x discount floor, and the $500 reserve floor are each currently duplicated across boss.md, accounting.md, and inventory.md. When I fixed the sign-off ambiguity, I had to edit boss.md and accounting.md together and keep them consistent — a real maintenance cost a single prompt wouldn't have.
  • Fewer total LLM calls and lower latency per ticket — ticket 101's first pass alone involved roughly a dozen tool calls spread across three separate agent contexts; a single agent doing the identical verification would plausibly need well under half that.

What a single agent loses, though, is the architectural guarantee this project cared about: separation of duties. With one agent holding every tool, there's no structural reason it couldn't call a money-moving tool itself — the "only Accounting can even attempt this, and even Accounting can only prepare it" split is a genuine safety property this system has that a single-agent version would have to re-create some other way (e.g. a tool-level allowlist keyed on some notion of "current role," which is really just reinventing agent boundaries from the inside).

✅ 4. Three problems this team could solve today, as-is

  1. A customer order for something actually in stock — e.g. 2x Yale Cap (OS; 40 on hand). Customer Service would confirm stock with check_inventory_stock and close it resolved in one hop, no Accounting involvement at all — the same code path that handled ticket 101's stock check, just with a "yes" instead of a "no."
  2. A rent notice with a wrong number in it — e.g. a landlord email claiming the rent is now $2,500. Facilities already has the exact rule for this (get_lease_status would show $2,400, a mismatch), and facilities.md explicitly tells it to flag the discrepancy and escalate rather than pay the claimed amount — this is a small variation on ticket 102, not a new capability.
  3. A small, in-policy invoice payment under $500 — unlike ticket 101's $840, a sub-$500 invoice wouldn't even need Boss's sign-off per accounting.md's own threshold; Accounting would verify it and call propose_payment directly, and a human would still approve it on the dashboard before it moved. This is the "happy path" for money that ticket 101 never actually exercised (it was always over the threshold).

🚧 5. Three problems this team could not solve, and what it would take

  1. Actually resolving ticket 103's shortfall (ship 8 now and backorder 12, place a new restock order, or decline). The team can diagnose this perfectly but has no tool to act on any resolution path.
    What's needed: a new MCP tool like create_purchase_order(vendor_id, sku, size, qty) (writing a new row somewhere real — an `invoices`-style table — not just a note), and a second kind of human-approval queue on the dashboard beyond payments, since "accept a partial order" is a real business decision a human should make, the same way approving a payment is.
  2. Actually telling the customer or landlord anything. Customer Service can compute exactly what Tauhid Zaman should be told, but no agent has a channel to tell him — there's no email/notification tool anywhere in this toolset.
    What's needed: wiring in something like the send_email tool already built elsewhere in this project (mcp_servers/outlook_mcp_server.py) as a new MCP tool for Customer Service/Facilities, plus — consistent with how payments work now — a human-approval step before any real email actually goes out, since sending the wrong message to a real customer is just as consequential as an unapproved payment.
  3. A ticket about a brand-new vendor or a product never sold before. Every tool in this system assumes the vendor/SKU already exists in vendors/inventory/pricing. Correctly, the agents would refuse to invent data about something not in the database rather than guess — but there's also no path for them to properly onboard a new vendor or product, since no create_vendor/add_sku tools exist.
    What's needed: a catalog-management tool set (create_vendor, add_sku_to_catalog), almost certainly behind another human-approval gate, and arguably a sixth agent ("Buyer" or "Merchandising") whose job is new-product/vendor onboarding — a different kind of work than triaging existing tickets, which is all the current five agents are built for.
